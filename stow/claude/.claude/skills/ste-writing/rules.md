# ASD-STE100 Issue 9 — Writing rules (Part 1, distilled)

Distilled from the official standard (ASD-STE100, Issue 9, January 2025 — asd-ste100.org).
Bold text is the rule statement, verbatim. The prose after it condenses the standard's guidance.
Sections 1-9 are normative STE rules. The General Recommendations (GR-1 to GR-8) are advisory, not STE rules.

## Section 1 – Words

### Rule 1.1 — Permitted word sources
**Use words that are: - Approved in the dictionary - Technical nouns - Technical verbs.**

STE has a controlled dictionary of the words most frequently used in technical writing. Words outside the dictionary are permitted only if they fit the defined technical noun or technical verb categories. A technical noun/verb is a term that refers to a specified concept or process in a subject field; the dictionary also lists unapproved words with alternatives.

### Rule 1.2 — One part of speech only
**Use approved words from the dictionary only as the specified part of speech.**

Each approved word carries a specified part of speech; use it only as that. Some words are approved as more than one part of speech (for example "clean" as verb and adjective), and position in the sentence shows the function. When an approved alternative has a different part of speech, change the sentence construction; never let a replacement change the meaning.

- STE: Do the leak test of the system. | non-STE: Test the system for leaks. ("test" is an approved noun, not a verb)

### Rule 1.3 — Approved meaning only
**Use approved words only with their approved meanings.**

Each approved word has one specified meaning, often more restricted than in standard English. Do not use an approved word with any other meaning.

- STE: Obey the safety instructions. | non-STE: Follow the safety instructions. ("follow" = come after, go after)

### Rule 1.4 — Approved verb and adjective forms
**Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.**

The dictionary lists each verb with its approved forms (infinitive/imperative, simple present, simple past, past participle as adjective) and each adjective in base form with comparative and superlative in parentheses where applicable. Adjectives that form comparatives with "more" and "most" have no listed forms, because "more" and "most" are approved words.

### Rule 1.5 — Technical noun categories
**You can use words that you can include in a technical noun category.**

The dictionary excludes technical nouns; take them from your company glossary or terminology database. A word is usable as a technical noun if it fits one or more of these 22 categories: 1) official parts information; 2) vehicles or machines and locations on them; 3) tools and support equipment, their parts and locations; 4) materials, consumables, and unwanted material; 5) facilities, infrastructure, and logistic procedures; 6) systems, components and circuits, their functions, configurations, and parts; 7) mathematical, scientific, engineering terms, and formulas; 8) navigation and geographic terms; 9) numbers, units of measurement and time (and their symbols); 10) quoted text; 11) professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities; 12) parts of the body; 13) common personal effects, food, and beverages; 14) medical terms; 15) official documents, parts of documentation, standards, and guidelines; 16) environmental and operational conditions; 17) colors; 18) damage terms; 19) computer science, information and communication technology; 20) civil and military operations; 21) law and regulations; 22) animals, plants, and other life forms. The category word lists are examples, not exhaustive. Colors count as technical nouns, and their comparative and superlative forms (blacker, the reddest) are not permitted.

### Rule 1.6 — Unapproved words as technical nouns
**Use a word that is not approved in the dictionary, only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun.**

A word listed as not approved becomes usable when it fits a technical noun category, and the same word can fit different categories in different contexts. A word not in the dictionary at all is also usable when it is part of a technical noun; do not substitute an approved alternative inside an established technical noun.

- STE: The base of the triangle is 5 cm. | non-STE: Make sure that the two spigots at the base of the unit engage. (use "bottom")

### Rule 1.7 — No technical nouns as verbs
**Do not use words that are technical nouns as verbs.**

Use a technical noun only as a noun, or as an adjective inside another technical noun. Rewrite the sentence instead. The same word may still be both, when it fits a noun category (rule 1.5) and a verb category (rule 1.12) — for example "drill".

- STE: Apply oil to the steel surfaces. | non-STE: Oil the steel surfaces.

### Rule 1.8 — Use established terms
**Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field.**

If an approved technical noun exists for a system, component, part, or process, use it. Such terms are usually in official parts information and company documentation.

### Rule 1.9 — Short, clear technical nouns
**When you must select a technical noun, use one which is short and easy to understand.**

When no approved term exists, choose one of not more than three words that is easy to understand. Where index numbers and illustrations identify the item, a bare noun is sufficient; otherwise add one or two adjectives.

- STE: Remove the four screws (10) that attach the flange (15) to the cover (20). | non-STE: Remove the four stainless steel pan head machine screws (10) that attach the metallic machined flange (15) to the front housing cover (20).

### Rule 1.10 — No regional, slang, or jargon
**Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.**

Words confined to a region, or technical slang and jargon, are unclear to readers from elsewhere and cause confusion. Always select well-known words.

- STE: After the firmware update, do not set the router to OFF. | non-STE: After the firmware update, do not brick the router.

### Rule 1.11 — One term per item
**Do not use different technical nouns for the same item.**

Once you select a technical noun for an item, use that same term throughout the text. Use the term approved in your company, industry, or subject field.

- STE: 1. Make sure that the actuator is in the open position. 3. Disconnect the actuator from the test rig. | non-STE: 1. ...the servo control unit... 3. ...the control unit...

### Rule 1.12 — Technical verb categories
**You can use verbs that you can include in a technical verb category.**

Technical verbs obey all other verb rules (section 3). A verb is usable if it fits one or more of these four categories: 1) manufacturing processes — a) remove material, b) add material, c) attach material, d) change mechanical strength/structure/physical properties, e) change surface finish, f) change shape; 2) computer processes and applications — a) input and output, b) user interface and application processes, c) system operations; 3) instructions and information for applicable subject fields — a) engineering/mathematical/scientific, b) medical, c) civil and military operations, d) navigation, e) automotive and railway, f) energy, oil, and gas; 4) law and regulations. Lists are examples only. If an approved dictionary verb gives the same instruction or information, use it instead; use a technical verb only when it is precise and correct in that context, and prefer an approved verb plus a technical noun where possible. An unapproved word may serve as a technical verb in its category context but not in other contexts.

- STE: Ream the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm. | non-STE: Machine the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm.

### Rule 1.13 — No technical verbs as nouns
**Do not use technical verbs as nouns.**

Use technical verbs only as verbs. The past participle form may be used as an adjective. The same word can be both a technical verb and a technical noun when it fits a category of each.

- STE: Ream the hole to a 0.20-inch dimension. | non-STE: Give the hole 0.20-inch ream.

### Rule 1.14 — American English spelling
**Use American English spelling unless other official directives tell you differently.**

Use the spelling given in the STE dictionary. Use a different spelling only when technical publication specifications, style guides, contracts, or other official directives require it. Do not change the spelling of quoted text (refer to rule 8.6).

- STE: Change the color of the display. | non-STE: Change the colour of the display.

## Section 2 – Multi-word nouns

### Rule 2.1 — Maximum three words
**Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words.**

A multi-word noun is a group of nouns and/or adjectives acting as one part of speech, with the head noun usually last. Long strings hide the relations between words and cause ambiguity, especially for non-native readers. Keep them to a maximum of three words, using prepositions ("of", "on", "in", "for") and explanation to break them up.

- STE: Calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection. | non-STE: Runway light connection resistance calibration. (5 words)

### Rule 2.2 — Long technical nouns
**When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full. Then, you can use one of these methods to make the technical noun clear: - Give a shorter form of the technical noun. - Use hyphens (-) between words that you use as one unit.**

Method 1: write the official term in full at its first occurrence, explain it if necessary, then use a shorter form (three words or less) or an approved abbreviation for the rest of the document; do not overload procedures with abbreviations, and do not abbreviate terms of three words or less. Method 2: hyphenate words that operate as one unit — hyphenated words always count as one word — but never hyphenate unrelated words, never build hyphenated groups of more than three words, do not add hyphens to approved terms of three words or less, and never remove a hyphen that is part of an approved technical noun.

- STE: Move the main-gear-door retraction-winch handle. (3 words) | non-STE: Move the main-gear-door-retraction-winch handle.

## Section 3 - Verbs

### Rule 3.1 — Dictionary verb forms
**Use only the verb forms that are given in the dictionary.**

The dictionary lists the permitted forms for each approved verb (for example REMOVE, REMOVES, REMOVED, REMOVED; GIVE, GIVES, GAVE, GIVEN).

### Rule 3.2 — Permitted tenses
**Use only these verb forms and tenses of verbs: - The infinitive form - The imperative form (command form) - The simple present tense - The simple past tense - The simple future tense - The past participle form (as an adjective).**

No other form or tense is permitted, in particular the present perfect (have/has adjusted), the past perfect (had adjusted), the present/past progressive (is/was adjusting), and all other complex verb constructions.

### Rule 3.3 — Past participle as adjective
**Use the past participle form as an adjective.**

Used as an adjective it shows a condition, and this is not passive voice. Put it before a noun, or after a form of "to be", "to become", or "to stay". Do not use a past participle form that is not in the dictionary; some approved adjectives (for example "permitted", "damaged") are past participles of verbs that are not approved and are usable as adjectives.

- STE: When the unit is fully disassembled, clean all the parts.

### Rule 3.4 — No auxiliary constructions
**Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.**

Do not combine the past participle with "have" or with other auxiliaries as a verb form; these produce unapproved tenses and passive sentences. Rewrite as simple past, as an imperative, or with the agent as subject.

- STE: The operator adjusted the linkage. | non-STE: The operator has adjusted the linkage.

### Rule 3.5 — Restricted "-ing" forms
**Use the "-ing" form of a verb only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical noun.**

Words ending in "-ing" can act as verbs, adjectives, nouns, or heads of long modifier groups and clauses, so they cause ambiguity and long sentences and are usually not permitted. They are permitted as technical nouns (Cleaning, Handling, Packaging, Shipping, Troubleshooting) and as modifiers in technical nouns related to function (air-conditioning system, grinding wheel, welding torch). Only a few approved dictionary words have an "-ing" form: nouns (lighting, opening, routing, servicing), adjectives (mating, missing, remaining), a pronoun (something), and a preposition (during).

- STE: When you do this procedure, obey all the safety precautions. | non-STE: When you are doing this procedure, obey all the safety precautions.

### Rule 3.6 — Active voice
**Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, you can use the passive voice only if the agent is unknown.**

In the active voice the grammatical subject is the agent that does the action; test for the passive by asking "by whom or by what?" (the preposition "by" is a strong indication). Four methods convert passive to active: 1) make the agent given after "by" the subject; 2) replace an infinitive construction with an active verb; 3) in procedural writing, use the imperative form; 4) when no agent is given, use "you" (the reader) or "we" (your company or organization) as the subject. For complex passive sentences with auxiliaries, decide whether you want a procedural sentence (imperative) or a descriptive sentence.

- STE: A switching relay connects the circuits. | non-STE: The circuits are connected by a switching relay.

### Rule 3.7 — Verbs, not nominalizations
**Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech.**

Verbs describe actions more clearly than nouns or other parts of speech, so prefer the direct verb where an approved one exists. If a word is not approved as a verb, do not use it as a verb — change the sentence construction instead.

- STE: Before you remove the unit, make sure that the power supply is OFF. | non-STE: Before the removal of the unit, make sure that the power supply is OFF.

## Section 4 – Sentences

### Rule 4.1 — Short, clear sentences
**Write short and clear sentences.**

Give accurate instructions and information. In procedures, address the reader directly in the imperative form and split long instructions into separate numbered steps. In descriptive text, give each sentence only one topic (subject or idea) and no imperative form, then build on that topic in the sentences that follow. Never be abstract: state what changes and by how much, and do not write anything inaccurate or open to more than one meaning.

- STE: When the temperature increases, the cure time will decrease. | non-STE: Different temperatures will change the cure time.

### Rule 4.2 — No omitted words or contractions
**Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.**

Each sentence must have all its parts, written in full. Do not omit nouns, verbs, subjects, or articles, and do not use contractions ("don't", "isn't", "aren't") — a shorter sentence made this way is not easier to read, and omitted articles cause ambiguity.

- STE: If shims are installed, remove them. | non-STE: If installed, remove the shims.

### Rule 4.3 — Vertical lists for complex text
**Use a vertical list for complex text.**

When a sentence must include many items or actions, put them in a vertical list. Rules: end the introductory sentence with a colon; identify each item with a number, letter, punctuation mark, or symbol (dash, bullet, letter, number); start each item with an uppercase letter; use an article before the noun that is the subject of each item where applicable; put a period at the end of an item only if it is a full sentence, and always at the end of the last item; never end an item with a comma or semicolon. Each item must connect clearly to the text before the colon, all items must be at the same level (avoid nested lists — put sub-items in parentheses instead), and procedural and descriptive writing must not be mixed in one list. In safety instructions, repeat the negative command (DO NOT) in each item rather than putting it in the lead-in.

- STE: `After the repair, do not use acetone to clean:` + `- The service cabinet` … | non-STE: `Do not use acetone for cleaning these parts after the repair:` + `- the service cabinet,` …

### Rule 4.4 — Connecting words and phrases
**Use connecting words and connecting phrases to connect sentences that contain related topics.**

Connecting words link a topic in one sentence to an idea in the next and give descriptive text a logical structure. Approved connecting words include "and", "but", "then", and "thus"; connecting phrases include "as a result" and "at the same time". Demonstrative adjectives can also act as connectors, and in procedures connecting words are used when an explanation follows a work step or to make safety instructions clear.

- STE: Tag the disassembled parts. This method will help you during the subsequent procedures.

### Rule 4.5 — Articles and demonstrative adjectives
**When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a noun or a multi-word noun.**

Articles show the position of nouns in the sentence; do not omit them to shorten the text. But do not use an article in general statements, concepts, or abstract qualities, and never use a definite article before a noun followed by an alphanumeric identifier (it is then a proper noun). In short sentences, use articles before all nouns; in a long series of items, use the article only before the first noun — and check that this does not make an adjective apply to more items than intended.

- STE: Tag circuit breaker 36L7. | non-STE: Tag the circuit breaker 36L7.
- STE: Install the new O-rings (15), the spacers (14), the nut (13) (only the O-rings are new) vs. Install the new O-rings (15), spacers (14), nut (13) (all are new).

## Section 5 – Procedural writing

### Rule 5.1 — Maximum 20 words
**Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.**

In procedures, the maximum sentence length is 20 words, and warnings, cautions, and other safety instructions must also obey this limit. Divide a longer instruction into two shorter sentences (in the same work step if the actions occur at the same time). Notes are the exception: a sentence in a note can have a maximum of 25 words.

- STE: Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole. Continue until the oil level is approximately 6 mm (0.24 in) below the surface of the flange cover. | non-STE: the same content as one 25-word sentence.

### Rule 5.2 — One instruction per sentence
**Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time.**

Write one instruction per sentence and show the sequence of work steps clearly, usually with numbers or letters; a procedure can have as many work steps as necessary. More than one instruction is permitted in a sentence when the actions occur at the same time, and a work step can have more than one sentence when actions occur at the same time or when a result occurs immediately after an action.

- STE: Cut and remove the wire. (simultaneous) | non-STE: Set the TEST switch to the middle position and release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch. (two separate steps)

### Rule 5.3 — Imperative form
**Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.**

Instructions must use the imperative; other structures cause ambiguity about whether a step matters, who did it, or who must do it. Do not use "must" before the imperative form unless the instruction is very important for safety or gives an important condition.

- STE: Remove oil and grease with a degreasing agent. | non-STE: Oil and grease are to be removed with a degreasing agent.

### Rule 5.4 — Condition before command
**When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a descriptive statement. Then, divide that descriptive statement from the command with a comma.**

State the condition first, then a comma, then the instruction, so the reader knows the condition before the action. The comma position is significant and can change the meaning of the sentence.

- STE: When the light comes on, set the switch to NORMAL. | non-STE: Set the switch to NORMAL when the light comes on.

### Rule 5.5 — Notes give information only
**Write notes only to give information, not instructions.**

Notes give information that helps the reader; they are descriptive text and obey the descriptive writing rules. A note must not give instructions, requirements, limits, tolerances, or the result of a work step (that information goes directly after the related action in the work step), and must not use the imperative form. A note can have one or more sentences, each a maximum of 25 words. Information needed to prevent damage or injury must be a warning or caution, not a note. Test your procedure by reading it without the notes: if the reader cannot do it correctly, move the missing information into a work step. Notes belong in procedures; use them in descriptions only for illustrations or tables.

- STE: NOTE: The gyroscope will become stable after approximately 15 seconds. | non-STE: NOTE: Make sure that the avionics ventilation system continues to operate correctly. (imperative — this is a work step)

## Section 6 – Descriptive writing

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions, so the imperative form of the verb is not permitted. It covers descriptions of an item, product, system or component; general information texts (reports, brochures, papers); and notes in a procedure.

### Rule 6.1 — Give information gradually
**Give information gradually.**

In descriptive text, release information step by step and make sure each sentence contains only one subject. Too much information too quickly forces the reader to read the text again.

- STE: "The system shows the pilot the deviations from the localizer course and the glideslope path." | non-STE: "During the approach, deviation pointers in the course indicators give commands to fly up or down and left or right, and this information comes from the VHF transceivers, which are part of the Instrument Landing System."

### Rule 6.2 — Key words and structure
**Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure.**

Key words and key phrases repeat across sentences to connect related ideas and show how information relates. Connecting words and phrases (for example "and," "but," "then," "thus," "as a result," "at the same time") act as traffic signs that tell the reader whether information is new, different, or a result. Do not change key words once chosen — the same terminology keeps the text clear.

### Rule 6.3 — Maximum 25 words
**Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.**

In descriptive writing the maximum sentence length is 25 words, longer than the procedural limit because descriptive text is more complex. Split longer sentences into two.

- STE: "A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other functions. It includes an operating system and an internet browser, and it can also operate software applications." (15 + 16 words) | non-STE: the same content as one 31-word sentence.

### Rule 6.4 — Paragraphs group related information
**Use paragraphs to show related information.**

Where procedures use numbers and letters for sequence, descriptive writing uses paragraphs to keep related information together and give logical order. A paragraph starts with a topic sentence that states the topic; the sentences that follow explain it or add related information.

### Rule 6.5 — One topic per paragraph
**Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic.**

Each paragraph describes exactly one topic. The topic sentence is the first and most important sentence: it gives new information and connects it logically to previous information, usually with a key word and/or a connecting word or phrase. Collected topic sentences should form a good outline of the whole text.

### Rule 6.6 — Maximum six sentences
**Make sure that no paragraph has more than six sentences.**

Paragraphs divide text into logical units and hold attention; they lose that function when too long. Do not put different topics in the same paragraph, and if a paragraph has more than six sentences, divide it into two smaller paragraphs.

## Section 7 – Safety instructions

Safety instructions tell readers that procedures or work steps can be dangerous or cause damage. Descriptive writing does not usually include them, unless the text is quoted or describes safety risks. Definitions: a **warning** tells the reader there is a risk of injury or death; a **caution** tells the reader there is a risk of damage to objects. Other domains may use different words (for example "danger," "attention," "notice") or graphical symbols; if you do, the content must still obey rules 7.1 thru 7.3. STE gives no formatting rules (uppercase in the examples is not a requirement) — follow the applicable style guides.

### Rule 7.1 — Identify the level of risk
**Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk.**

Use a word, or where applicable a symbol, that immediately shows the reader the level of risk. Do an accurate risk analysis, then decide: risk of injury or death → warning; risk of damage to machines, tools or equipment → caution; both levels together → warning. Name the hazard and its consequence in concrete words rather than making an abstract general statement.

- STE: "WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE OXYGEN TUBES ARE FULLY CLEAN. OXYGEN AND GREASE MAKE AN EXPLOSIVE MIXTURE. AN EXPLOSION CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH." | non-STE: "CAUTION: EXTREME CLEANLINESS OF OXYGEN TUBES IS IMPERATIVE."

### Rule 7.2 — Start with command or condition
**Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition.**

The first element must be a command (imperative) so the reader knows how to prevent the accident. If the reader must know a condition before the procedure or work step starts, give that condition first.

- STE: "DO NOT SWALLOW THE SOLVENT." / "WHILE YOU USE THE SPRAY PAINT, POINT THE SPRAY AWAY FROM YOUR FACE."

### Rule 7.3 — Explain the risk or result
**Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result.**

Whenever possible, tell the reader what problems can occur if the safety instruction is not obeyed. A clear, specified risk makes the person doing the task more careful. The explanation follows the command or condition.

- STE: "CAUTION: WHEN YOU ASSEMBLE THE UNIT, DO NOT LET THE PARTS FALL. IF THEY FALL, PERMANENT DAMAGE TO THE PARTS CAN OCCUR."

## Section 8 – Punctuation and word count

STE gives no general punctuation rules beyond those below; refer to standard style manuals for the rest.

### Rule 8.1 — No semicolon
**You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;).**

The semicolon is forbidden because it permits very long sentences and is not easy to use correctly. Always write two separate sentences instead.

- STE: "Examine the removed parts for damage. Replace the damaged part(s)." | non-STE: "Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones."

### Rule 8.2 — Hyphens connect related words
**Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.**

Hyphenate: (1) two-or-more-word terms used as adjectives before a noun (low-altitude flight, quick-release fastener, up-to-date information); (2) two-word fractions and numbers (forty-seven, three-sixteenths); (3) an uppercase letter or number plus a noun giving shape or configuration (O-ring, L-shaped bracket, 3-prong connector, 180-grit abrasive cloth); (4) verbs whose first part is a noun or other part of speech (heat-treat, arc-weld, short-circuit); (5) prefixes ending in a vowel before a root starting with a vowel (de-icing, anti-icing, pre-amplifier). A hyphen is not a dash — a dash divides ideas, shows a range, or signals a pause.

### Rule 8.3 — Permitted uses of parentheses
**You can use parentheses: - To make references to illustrations or text - To include letters or numbers that identify items on an illustration or in a text - To identify the work steps in a procedure - To include abbreviations - To give the singular and plural forms of a noun at the same time - To explain words or a part of a sentence - To include an alternative.**

These seven uses are the permitted ones. Examples: "Remove the valve (10, Figure 1)."; "Disconnect the hoses (2) and (12)."; "(1) Install the locking cap (4)."; "A Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) ..."; "Do the applicable test(s)."; "Increase the pressure slowly (not more than 10 psi each minute)."; "Open the left (right) access panel L42 (R42)."

### Rule 8.4 — Colon ends a sentence in lists
**In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence.**

The colon that introduces a vertical list counts as a full stop: a maximum of 20 words before it in procedural sentences and 25 words in descriptive sentences. Each list item after the colon counts as a new sentence, with the same limits — 20 words procedural, 25 words descriptive.

### Rule 8.5 — Parentheses count as one word
**When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence.**

Text in parentheses counts as one word of the host sentence, but the parenthetical words also form a separate sentence and are counted there. Identifiers in parentheses (a number, a letter, an alphanumeric identifier) and abbreviations in parentheses also count as one word.

- STE: "Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released (the EMER legend is off)." = 10 words; the parenthetical sentence has 5 words.

### Rule 8.6 — Elements counted as one word
**Count each of these elements as one word: - Numbers - Numbers together with units of measurement - Abbreviations - Alphanumeric identifiers - Quoted text - Titles, headings, and text on placards and labels - Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities.**

Details: numbers count as one word each ("twenty-one" = 1 word), but do not count numbers that identify paragraphs or work steps. A number with its unit counts as one word together ("10 °C" = 1 word, "20 kilograms" = 1 word). Each abbreviation, acronym or initialism counts as one word (NASA, VPN, "10 a.m."). Each alphanumeric identifier counts as one word ("No. 1", "36L7"). Quoted text counts as one word, including text shown by uppercase letters or a different font, and formulas. Titles, headings, and placard or label text — words you cannot change — count as one word each. Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations and geopolitical entities count as one word each ("United States of America", "George Washington").

- STE: "Refer to Testing and Fault Isolation, page block 1001." = 6 words (the section title counts as one word).

### Rule 8.7 — Hyphenated words count as one
**Hyphenated words count as one word.**

Word groups that are not normally adjectives but function as an adjective before a noun are hyphenated and count as one word. Hyphenated groups used to clarify long technical nouns also count as one word.

- STE: "Clean the surface with a soap-and-water solution." = 7 words; "Main-gear-door retraction-winch handle" = 3 words.

## Section 9 – Writing practices

### Rule 9.1 — Rewrite, don't swap words
**Use a different sentence construction to write a sentence when a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient.**

The dictionary gives approved alternatives; if an alternative has the same part of speech and does not change the meaning, substitute it directly. Rewrite the sentence instead when: the grammatical structure must change to fit the alternative, a direct swap gives a meaningless result, the alternative changes the meaning, or the word is absent from the dictionary. Rewriting can mean different verb forms, split sentences, removal of unnecessary information, or moving repeated information into a table heading — and after any change, check the effect on the rest of the text.

- STE: During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge. | non-STE: The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test.

### Rule 9.2 — Approved meaning and part of speech
**Use each approved word correctly.**

Approved words usually have only one approved meaning (read the approved meaning column); other standard-English meanings are not approved. Use each word only as its approved part of speech — for example "work" is a noun only, "help" a verb only, "damage" a noun only. A few important words are approved as more than one part of speech (for example "flush" as verb and adjective), where position and context make the function obvious.

- STE: Use (or put on) protective clothing. | non-STE: Wear protective clothing. ("Wear" is approved only as "to become damaged by friction".)

### Rule 9.3 — No new phrasal verbs
**When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs.**

A verb plus one or more prepositions can form a phrasal verb whose meaning differs from its parts, which causes ambiguity; combining approved words into such a phrase is not permitted. Phrasal verbs are usually not listed as "not approved" in the dictionary, so check that each word carries its dictionary meaning. Only a small number of phrasal verbs (for example "put on", "come on") are approved, and all have a restricted meaning.

- STE: After you extinguish the fire, close the valve. | non-STE: After you put out the fire, close the valve.

### Rule 9.4 — Consistent terminology and wording
**When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style.**

The same item must always get the same noun, and the same type of work step must always get the same wording, even when two variants are both correct STE. Varied wording for identical actions causes confusion and delays; in descriptive text, consistent words plus a logical structure let the reader understand the text as a unit.

- STE: Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the two bolts. | non-STE: Lubricate the two bolts with oil. (when the other steps already use the "apply oil to the threads" wording)

## General recommendations (advisory)

### GR-1 — The conjunction "that"

Not a rule, but a recommendation. Use the conjunction "that" as much as possible after verbs such as "make sure", "show", and "recommend": it marks where the main clause ends and the subordinate clause starts, prevents ambiguity, and helps translation because many languages cannot omit the equivalent word.

- WRITE: Make sure that the valve is open. | Do not write: Make sure the valve is open.

### GR-2 — The preposition "with"

"With" has three approved meanings — association or relationship, help or sharing, and a means or instrument — so a sentence such as "Install the panel with the green fasteners" can be read three ways. Read any sentence containing "with" again and check that the meaning is unambiguous and unchanged; put a condition first rather than trailing it with "with". If you restructure to avoid "with", keep the primary action verb in the work step ("Seal the opening with tool TS9867", not "Use tool TS9867 to seal the opening").

- WRITE: When you supply hydraulic pressure, make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1). | Do not write: Make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1) with hydraulic pressure supplied.

### GR-3 — How to use pronouns

Use only the pronouns listed in the dictionary; pronouns not in it (for example "she" or "he") are not permitted. If a pronoun could refer to more than one noun, replace it with the noun it refers to.

- WRITE: If you engage the pins incorrectly with the seats, the pins can become damaged. | Do not write: ... they can become damaged.

### GR-4 — The pronoun "this"

Make sure the reader knows which item "this" refers to; if it could refer to more than one item, state the context again or rewrite the sentence.

- WRITE: Make sure that the cover is not locked. If the cover is locked, this can cause damage to the probe. | Do not write: Make sure that the cover is not locked (this can cause damage to the probe).

### GR-5 — False friends

A false friend is a word that looks like one in the writer's native language but means something different in English. Non-native speakers must confirm the English meaning of a word before using it.

- WRITE: When you use this adhesive, obey the manufacturer's instructions. | Do not write: Obey the dispositions of the manufacturer when you use this adhesive.

### GR-6 — Latin abbreviations

Do not use Latin abbreviations such as "e.g.", "i.e.", and "etc.", because readers who do not know them are confused. Use the English words ("for example", "that is", "and so on"), or omit the abbreviation when it is not necessary.

- WRITE: Discard the standard parts (for example, washers, bolts, and nuts) each time you remove them. | Do not write: ... (e.g., washers, screws, bolts, and nuts) ...

### GR-7 — Inclusive language

Always use gender-neutral language. Gender-specific pronouns such as "he" and "she" are not permitted in STE, and "man" and "woman" are not permitted unless necessary in the applicable context (for example a medical text). Use neutral terms and constructions to prevent bias; refer to external inclusive-language guidance for more detail.

### GR-8 — Possessive form

The possessive form (apostrophe + "s", the Saxon genitive) is permitted in STE, but use it only when you are sure the sentence is correct — otherwise avoid it, because many other languages have no equivalent and non-native readers can find it hard to understand. Always defer to publication specifications, style guides, or other official directives.
